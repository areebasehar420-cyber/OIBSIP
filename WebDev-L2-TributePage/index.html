Marie Curie

She chased invisible light and changed what we know about matter.

A life in the laboratory

Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw in 1867, when women in Russian-ruled Poland were barred from university. She studied in secret classes and saved money for years so she could travel to Paris. In 1891 she enrolled at the Sorbonne, living on very little and working until late in cold rented rooms.

In Paris she met Pierre Curie, a physicist, and they married in 1895. Inspired by Henri Becquerel's finding that uranium gives off mysterious rays, Marie began measuring that radiation carefully. She noticed that some ores were far more active than their uranium content explained, and concluded that they must hide new elements.

Processing tonnes of ore in a draughty shed, the Curies announced polonium, named for her homeland, and then radium in 1898. She coined the word "radioactivity" for the phenomenon. In 1903 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physics, becoming the first woman to win a Nobel. After Pierre's sudden death in 1906 she took over his teaching post and became the Sorbonne's first female professor.

Her 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry made her the first person honoured in two scientific fields. During the First World War she helped equip vehicles with X-ray machines and trained operators so surgeons could find shrapnel in wounded soldiers. She died in 1934 from a blood disease most likely linked to her years of exposure to radiation, leaving behind a lasting legacy of research and of doors opened for women in science.
